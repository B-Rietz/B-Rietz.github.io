Warp Factories review inbox · Self-initiated concept + prototype

Trusting the factory floor

Coding agents are moving from one-at-a-time helpers to pipelines that triage, build, review, and verify in parallel. As that happens, the human bottleneck moves to understanding and trusting the work. This concept is a risk-first inbox: it shows a developer what actually needs them, and lets them decide in place only when the evidence is on screen.

The question

With five agents running, what deserves my attention?

I ran a pipeline like this myself at Lennar (see the case study). The hard part was never starting agents. It was knowing what each one was doing, which results were safe to accept, and which moment actually needed me.

Hypothesis: review should be risk-first, not diff-first. Show intent and evidence up front, batch the safe work, and send the risky work to the top.

What I found

I set up a real factory on this portfolio and gave it a real task.

I connected Warp Factories to this site's repository and asked it to add a keyboard skip link. It opened a pull request, I asked for a revision, and I merged it. Then I compared what I saw against six agent tools.

1

The agent knew where I should look. The inbox hid it.

The agent's summary ended with exactly what a human should check: "the homepage target is the hero header, since that page has no <main>." In the inbox, that sentence sat at the end of a summary cut off at two lines. The most useful signal was the least visible.

Friction log #8 · my factory run, Nov 2026
2

Raw diff size lies about risk.

My revision showed as +138 / −112 on GitHub. Ignoring whitespace, it was five lines: the agent had re-indented the page. A tiny, safe change looked like the riskiest thing in the queue.

Friction log #10 · PR #1
3

No tool ranks parallel work by what needs a human.

Across Warp, Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Copilot, and Devin, progress is a timeline and approval is either per action or per pull request. The evidence that makes a change trustworthy (tests, browser checks) is in the logs, not on the review screen.

Competitor scan · six tools, primary docs
4

Deciding means leaving.

The inbox's only action on my pull request was "Mark as resolved." To approve, I had to go to GitHub. Every decision cost a context switch.

Key decisions

What I chose, and why

1

Three sections, not a score

"Needs you," "Review when ready," and "Routine." A score hides its reasons and invites false confidence. Sections give a clear stopping point, and reason tags on every row make each placement easy to argue with.

2

Risk comes from evidence, not size

Sensitive paths (payments, auth, CI) or failed or missing checks put an item in "Needs you." Missing evidence counts as risk. Change size only ranks items within a section, and it's measured ignoring whitespace, so a re-indent never looks dangerous.

3

The agent's review focus is on every row

One line: "Check: …". It tells you where to look, not whether to worry, so it never raises an item's risk on its own.

4

You can act only where you can see enough to decide

Routine rows carry enough evidence to approve right from the list. Anything riskier has to be opened first, and approve lives next to the evidence. Where a button appears is itself a trust signal. A button on a row you haven't understood trains people to rubber-stamp.

5

Progressive disclosure, three layers

The row helps you decide whether to open it. The pane shows intent, review focus, checks, and the meaningful diff, which is enough to approve. Below that, in its own scrolling area, sit the full diff, video proof, timeline, and cost, always there for when you want more.

6

Fast, but never blind

Keyboard-first throughout, because that's how this audience works. Batch approval shows exactly what's included and gives you an undo window. If an item's evidence changes after you've seen it, it moves up with a one-line reason instead of quietly re-sorting.

What I'd measure

Faster decisions, without more mistakes.

  • Time to decision per item, from arriving in the inbox to approve or request changes.
  • Share of routine work batch-approved, so attention goes where it's needed.
  • Reversal rate: approved changes later reverted. This is the guardrail against false confidence.
  • Context switches per decision: how often you had to leave for the run or GitHub.
